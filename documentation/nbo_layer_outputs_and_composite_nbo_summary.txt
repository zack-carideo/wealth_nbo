The Mortgage outcome is the clearest illustration of why all four signals matter together. Mortgage has the lowest propensity of the four candidates (0.18) and a moderate timing score, so a propensity-only model would never recommend it as top offer — but its strong sequence alignment (this customer's portfolio state has 0.45 transition probability to mortgage) and very high expected value ($4,800) push it to the top of the composite ranking. That's the entire business case for the framework in one row: orthogonal signals that disagree with each other often produce the most economically valuable recommendation.
A few things worth knowing about how this composite behaves in practice:
The multiplication is unforgiving of zeros — if any single signal is near zero for a (customer, product) pair, the composite collapses, which is actually the desired behavior because near-zero propensity, sequence, or timing each independently mean "don't recommend this." The flip side is that it's also unforgiving of bad calibration: if any layer systematically over- or under-estimates probabilities, the composite inherits that bias multiplicatively. Calibration checks per layer become non-optional, which is part of why "Calibration check" lives in the Propensity Optimal column.
The composite score's units are dollars (because value is in dollars and the other three signals are dimensionless probabilities), which is convenient for business communication — you can say "this customer is worth $272 in expected next-product revenue." That's a much more actionable number for partner conversations than a unitless score in [0,1].
